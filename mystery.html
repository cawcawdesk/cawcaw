THE MYSTERY OF THE MISSING KILOMETRE

The Mystery of the Missing Kilometre on Ancient India’s Imperial Royal Road — from royal caravans to modern traffic, one of India’s oldest roads refuses to disappear.
By Navdeep Sood • 2026 • 7 Minute Read
A kilometre may appear insignificant on a map.
A small line.
A short distance.
Something easily ignored.
But sometimes, a missing kilometre can open a forgotten chapter of history.

Some of India’s oldest roads do not announce their age. Modern traffic moves across them, villages have grown around them and fields have replaced much of the landscape that once surrounded them.

Yet occasionally, the road reveals a clue that forces history to be looked at again.

Between Fatehabad Sarai and Sarai Amanat Khan in Tarn Taran, Punjab — two sites included in the UNESCO Tentative List for the historic Uttarapath–Badshahi Sadak–Grand Trunk Road — two Kos Minars were already listed in the records.

But fieldwork along the historic road revealed six more, bringing the total to eight surviving Kos Minars.

Some were surprisingly well hidden, concealed within settlements and areas that no longer resemble the landscape of the old imperial highway.

Identifying them became one of the most fascinating findings of the field survey.

Surviving Kos Minars provide physical clues to the measurement system of the historic road.

I. The Distance Markers of an Empire

These towers were not decorative monuments.

They were the distance markers of the imperial highway, helping travellers understand the stages of a long journey.

A traveller moving through the landscape centuries ago could encounter a brick tower and know that another measured stage of the journey had been reached.

The road carried its own measurement system.

The Kos Minars therefore represented something more than individual monuments.

Together, they formed a physical language of distance across the historic route.

II. The Kilometre That Did Not Fit

But there is something unusual about their spacing.

The kos is commonly described today as approximately 3.2 km.

That is the figure most often associated with the distance represented by a Kos Minar.

Yet the measurements between the surviving towers on this particular stretch of the old Delhi–Lahore road repeatedly come close to another value.

About 4.2 kilometres.

That is not a random difference.

The repeated spacing raised a much more interesting possibility: perhaps the towers were preserving a different historical standard of the kos.

The spatial pattern of surviving Kos Minars points towards a recurring distance of approximately 4.2 km.

III. The Akbari Kos Appears

History already carried a clue.

The Akbari kos.

The Ain-i-Akbari describes a kos in terms of 5,000 Ilahi gaz. Using that historical measure, the study calculates an Akbari kos of approximately 4.19 km.

Suddenly, the landscape begins to make sense.

The measurements between the surviving towers come remarkably close to the Akbari standard.

The towers appear to tell us something that a modern summary of the kos can easily hide.

The distance represented by a historic Kos Minar was not necessarily the same everywhere or across every historical measurement tradition.

On this section of the old road, the physical evidence points towards the Akbari standard.

The road itself appears to preserve the measurement.

IV. When Monuments Became Mathematical Evidence

The discovery is simple but significant.

The Kos Minars were built to help people understand distance.

Centuries later, their surviving locations can themselves be measured to investigate the system they represented.

A monument becomes evidence.

A distance becomes historical data.

And a road becomes an archive.

This is where field archaeology, historical research and GIS begin to meet.

The researchers documented the surviving structures and examined their spatial relationships rather than treating each tower as an isolated monument.

The key clue is not simply that one pair of Kos Minars happens to be about 4.2 km apart. The significance comes from the repeated pattern across the surviving markers and its close correspondence with the calculated Akbari kos of approximately 4.19 km.

V. Two Long Gaps

The map contained another clue.

Most consecutive Kos Minars followed the approximately 4.2 km pattern.

But two intervals were unusually long.

Approximately 8.4 km.

That was almost twice the dominant spacing.

The study interpreted these unusually long intervals as possible locations where an intermediate Kos Minar may once have existed but is no longer visible or recorded.

The missing structures may have disappeared.

But their possible positions still leave a trace in the geometry of the surviving monuments.

Long intervals in the surviving sequence may indicate locations of intermediate Kos Minars that are no longer visible or recorded.

VI. An Imperial Road Older Than Its Empire

The story may also be older than the Mughals.

The great north Indian road corridor had been important for centuries, with its deeper history reaching back to organised communication networks associated with Chandragupta Maurya.

Successive rulers inherited and strengthened routes whose geographical importance outlasted individual empires.

The Mughals gave parts of this transportation landscape an elaborate imperial infrastructure.

The surviving Kos Minars allow us to glimpse one small but fascinating part of that much longer story.

The road therefore cannot be understood only as a Mughal creation.

It belongs to a much deeper tradition of movement across northern India.

VII. The Road Still Counts

For the modern traveller, this transforms an ordinary drive through Punjab into something much more intriguing.

The road may look modern.

The traffic certainly is.

The settlements have changed.

The landscape has changed.

But beneath the movement of the present lies an ancient imperial transportation system in which distance was physically built into the landscape.

Today, a traveller asks a navigation app:

“How far is the next destination?”

Centuries ago, the answer stood beside the road.

And in this corner of Punjab, those ancient markers appear to have been counting the journey in approximately 4.2-kilometre Akbari kos, rather than the familiar approximately 3.2-kilometre figure.

WHY THIS MATTERS

The importance of the finding is not simply the difference between two numbers. It shows how surviving monuments can preserve evidence of historical systems of measurement and movement that might otherwise remain abstract.

VIII. When the Landscape Becomes the Archive

We often search for history in manuscripts.

In inscriptions.

In famous monuments.

But sometimes history survives in the space between things.

A tower remembers distance.

A road remembers movement.

A repeated interval preserves a pattern.

And a missing structure may leave behind a mathematical shadow.

The Kos Minars of Tarn Taran turn an ordinary stretch of Punjab into a remarkable historical measuring instrument.

The road survived.

The empire disappeared.

But its measurement is still standing.

Sometimes history is not hidden inside ruins.

Sometimes it is hidden in the distance between them.

THE LAST CAW


The road survived.

The empire disappeared.

But its measurement is still standing.

Centuries ago, travellers looked to these towers to understand how far they had come.

Today, we look at them to understand how the past measured the journey.
